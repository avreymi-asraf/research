ABSTRACT
Self-distillation is an emerging approach that trains a model to imitate its own
predictions when conditioned on privileged information, such as correct answers
or environmental feedback. Recent methods have reported better sample effi-
ciency and generalization than reinforcement learning (RL), but it remains un-
clear whether these gains extend beyond in-distribution evaluations and whether
they stem from privileged distillation. In this paper, we revisit two representa-
tive methods, Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) and On-Policy Self-
Distillation (OPSD), across code, science, tool use, and math reasoning tasks.
We find that their reported gains do not consistently generalize to unseen prob-
lems and are largely explained by overfitting or transferring existing reasoning
behavior, rather than effectively using privileged information. Moreover, self-
distillation gradients show weak alignment with policy gradients that directly
optimize expected reward, questioning whether they provide an effective direc-
tion for policy improvement. Our experiments across Qwen3 models (1.7B–14B)
and OLMo-3-7B suggest that effectively leveraging privileged information to im-
prove reasoning remains an open challenge. Our code is available at https:
//github.com/sleepypandu/does-sd-improve-reasoning.

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has be-
come a standard approach for finetuning large language models
(LLMs) on reasoning tasks such as mathematics and coding (Lam-
bert et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). As responses in these do-
mains are automatically verifiable, scaling RLVR is straightfor-
ward. However, typical RLVR pipelines use binary rewards that
indicate only whether the final answer is correct or whether all unit
tests pass. Such rewards provide no information on how attempted
solutions should be revised. Process reward models score individ-
ual reasoning steps (Wang et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2025), but require
training additional models.
A recently popularized alternative to RLVR is self-distillation,
which requires neither an external stronger model nor an explicit
reward function. It exploits the fact that reasoning tasks often come
with feedback far richer than the binary outcome that RLVR con-
sumes, such as reference solutions, critiques, error messages, or
failing unit tests. In self-distillation, the model samples a trace and
is trained to match, at each token along that trace, the distribution of
a (teacher) copy of itself that additionally conditions on this privi-
leged information (H¨ubotter et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026; Zhao
et al., 2026a). The self-conditioned teacher converts rich but un-
structured feedback into a dense, per-token training signal.
However, prior work questions the validity of self-distillation objectives as a mechanism to improve
the student policy. The self-conditioned teacher’s outputs do not express uncertainty through tokens

We revisit this question through careful reproductions of self-distillation policy optimization (SDPO)
(H¨ubotter et al., 2026) and on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026a). We leverage
Qwen3 (Qwen Team, 2025) and Olmo-3 (Olmo Team, 2025) models to ensure our observations
hold over a range of model sizes and families. We find that the reported gains from privileged-
information self-distillation in reasoning domains are weaker than they appear:
• SDPO’s coding gains do not transfer to unseen problems (Section 4.1). SDPO’s Live-
CodeBench v6 (Jain et al., 2024) results train and evaluate on the same set of coding problems.
Correcting for this reveals that SDPO overfits to its training problems, reducing the diversity of
its solutions (Figure 1), and degrades on held-out problems while policy-gradient training on the
same verifiable reward generalizes.
• SDPO’s gains in science reasoning and tool use do not generalize out of domain (Section 4.2).
Across five science-reasoning (Feng et al., 2024) and tool-use (Tang et al., 2023) domains, SDPO
and GRPO in-domain performances are similar, but in four of five it lowers accuracy on the held-
out domains, while GRPO preserves it.
• OPSD transfers thinking-mode behavior, not privileged information (Section 4.3). In math
reasoning, privileged information barely changes the self-conditioned teacher’s token distribution
(Figure 2), and distilling thinking-mode behavior has a substantially larger effect than privileged
information within OPSD’s reported results.
• Self-distillation gradients do not align with policy gradients (Section 4.4). Prior work moti-
vates self-distillation as approximate policy improvement by arguing that its gradient aligns with
the policy gradient (Shenfeld et al., 2026). This alignment matters because a small update im-
proves expected reward only to the extent that it follows the policy gradient (Section 3.1). We
therefore measure how well each method’s training gradient aligns with the policy gradient on
held-out problems. On Qwen3 models, OPSD and SDPO gradients are consistently less aligned
than GRPO gradients computed on the same training problems and often point away from the
held-out policy gradient altogether.

2 RELATED WORKS
Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR). The goal of reinforcement learning
is to maximize the expectation of reward under a policy’s responses. For verifiable tasks in math
reasoning and coding, such rewards are assigned by verifying the equivalence between expressions
or checking whether a proposed program passes all test-cases. RLVR for LLMs is typically done
using policy gradient algorithms (Williams, 1992; Sutton et al., 2000). Although PPO (Schulman
et al., 2017) was used in foundational work (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022), critic-free methods
such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and RLOO (Kool et al., 2019; Ahmadian et al., 2024) have become
popular due to their simplicity, despite feedback only being given at the trajectory level.
On-policy distillation (OPD). Unlike classic distillation, which tries to match teacher-generated
outputs (Hinton et al., 2015), on-policy distillation seeks to boost performance by minimizing per-
token divergences between student logits π

merging the capabilities of multiple domain-specialist expert models (Xu et al., 2026; Kimi Team,
2026; Ma et al., 2026).
Self-distillation and privileged information. Instead of relying on an external stronger model,
self-distillation (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026a; H¨ubotter et al., 2026) parameterizes the

higher-reward policy, transferring the reference solution’s reasoning content.
Limitations of self-distillation. Prior work documents several failure modes of self-distillation:
SDPO generalizes poorly out of distribution and in continual learning settings (Wang et al., 2026),
self-distillation degrades thinking models by suppressing backtracking behavior and collapsing re-
sponse lengths (Kaur et al., 2026; Harne et al., 2026), and the self-conditioned teacher fails to dis-
tinguish correct from incorrect student solutions (Nguyen et al., 2026). Concurrently, Shrestha &
Tessier (2026) show that OPSD’s gains stem from thinking-mode transfer rather than privileged in-
formation. Methods that retain GRPO and use the teacher only to reweight advantages such as Pan
et al. (2026) fall outside our scope, as we study the self-distillation objective in isolation. We provide
an extended discussion of existing work on the limitations of self-distillation, and of how our paper
differs from each, in Appendix A.

Proposition 3.1 would require of an optimal teacher (Section 4.4).

4.1 SDPO DOES NOT GENERALIZE TO NEW CODING PROBLEMS
Harne et al. (2026) suggest that the self-conditioned teacher is heavily biased towards the reference
solution given in context in a way that does not generalize, which makes the coding gains reported
by SDPO (H¨ubotter et al., 2026) surprising. We observe that SDPO’s coding evaluations reuse its
training problems, thus not cleanly measuring generalization. We hypothesize that SDPO’s coding
gains stem from this discrepancy.
SDPO builds its teacher from privileged information available only at training time. On coding tasks,
the teacher sees a successful student rollout from the same batch along with environment feedback
on execution errors on test cases. They claim that SDPO improves 7.6% over GRPO baselines in
accuracy on LiveCodeBench v6 (Jain et al., 2024) evaluations. We construct a clean split of the
LCB data, reproduce SDPO overall gains on their data split, and observe that SDPO does not lead
to performance gains when evaluated on an unseen test set.

in training, while GRPO improves gradually over all 200 steps (Figure 6). By step 200, the two
methods are within one standard deviation of each other in biology, material, and physics (SDPO

where SDPO leads (77.7 vs. 68.9), and tool use, where GRPO leads (53.5 vs. 66.3) (Table 1).

ence domains lowers average out-of-domain accuracy by 7.1 to 16.6 points, whereas GRPO slightly
improves it (+0.9 to +1.6; Table 1). Training on tool use is the exception, with SDPO slightly
improving out-of-domain accuracy (+0.9), with GRPO slightly lowering it (−2.1). The drops are
largest when evaluating on material, physics, and tool use, where SDPO’s accuracy peaks early in
training and then declines steadily (Figure 6). SDPO’s in-domain gains thus come at the cost of
out-of-domain performance that policy-gradient training generally preserves.

4.3 OPSD GAINS DEPEND ON THINKING MODE TRANSFER, NOT PRIVILEGED INFORMATION
OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026a) reports gains on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25 (Mathematical As-
sociation of America, 2024; Balunovi´c et al., 2025) by distilling a thinking-on Qwen3 teacher con-
ditioned on a reference solution z into the same model with thinking off (Appendix C). The teacher
thus differs from the student in two ways: it sees z, and it thinks.
We therefore seek to isolate the influence of the content of z with the teacher thinking mode. If
OPSD gains come from conditioning on z, replacing it with the reference solution of a different
problem or removing it entirely should degrade performance.
Setup. Following Zhao et al. (2026a), we train on OpenThoughts (Guha et al., 2025) math problems,
each paired with a reference solution. We fix the student to thinking-off and ablate the teacher’s
thinking mode (on or off) and its privileged information: the problem’s own reference solution (ref
z), no reference solution (no z), or the reference solution of a different problem (shuffled z). The
teacher template is otherwise fixed, so the three differ only in the content of z.
Zhao et al. (2026a) train Qwen3-1.7B to 8B with OPSD for 100 steps and compare against thinking-
on GRPO (500 steps) and SFT (100 steps). To evaluate fully converged performance, we train
OPSD and SFT for 200 steps and GRPO, with thinking on and off, for 500 steps, and evaluate on
the same datasets with thinking on and off. As Shenfeld et al. (2026); H¨ubotter et al. (2026) sug-
gest self-distillation works better for larger models, we also test Qwen3-14B and a second model
family, Olmo-3-7B-Instruct. As the latter has no thinking toggle, we instead compare two teachers
given the same privileged information: the student itself (self-distillation) and Olmo-3-7B-Think,

5 LIMITATIONS
Our experiments are limited to the Qwen3 and Olmo model families and the SDPO and OPSD self-
distillation recipes. We do not address the more recent developments on self-distillation techniques,
which include harnesses (Zhao et al., 2026b), contrastive formulations (Pan et al., 2026), or more
complex aggregation of feedback from neighboring rollouts (Yu et al., 2026), which may combat
the limitations of these self-distillation methods. Moreover, we focus on verifiable domains and
acknowledge that self-distillation has use cases in non-verifiable settings (Buening et al., 2026).
6 CONCLUSION
We show that the gains attributed to privileged self-distillation either do not generalize or do not
come from privileged information. SDPO’s improvements on LiveCodeBench v6 are confined
to problems seen during training, and its gains in other domains are come at the cost of out-of-
distribution performance. OPSD’s math gains rely on thinking-mode transfer, with privileged infor-

mal teacher. Together, these results highlight the need to develop principled reinforcement learning
methods that can exploit privileged information.

A EXTENDED RELATED WORKS
We divide existing work on the limitations of self-distillation techniques into the following:
Empirical re-evaluations. Wang et al. (2026) demonstrate that SDPO struggles to generalize to
out-of-distribution domains, and fail in continual learning settings where GRPO succeeds. Kaur
et al. (2026) highlight that self-distillation degrades thinking-models; with previous works primarily
focusing on instruction-tuned models. Concurrent to our work, Shrestha & Tessier (2026); Zhang
et al. (2026); Ichihara et al. (2026) show that OPSD does not rely on privileged information, but on
thinking-mode transfer. In contrast to these works, we provide our results with an exact reproduction
of SDPO and OPSD training and evaluating settings, and we ask whether SDPO’s gains in other
domains reflect improvements in reasoning.
Token-level symptoms. Kim et al. (2026); Kaur et al. (2026); Pan et al. (2026); Harne et al. (2026)
show that self-distillation losses concentrate on tokens which express uncertainty and have high
entropy. This results in a suppression of the student’s backtracking behavior and collapse in response
lengths. Instead, we focus on the positive results reported by OPSD, and find through token-level
diagnoses and response length metrics, that performance improvements relate to thinking-mode
transfer.
Sequence-level diagnoses. Nguyen et al. (2026) develop statistics that indicate that the self-
conditioned teacher cannot differentiate between correct and incorrect solutions generated by the
student. Harne et al. (2026) present analogous results, showing that the self-conditioned teacher is
greatly biased towards the reference solution it is provided, mirroring Yang et al. (2026)’s results
indicating that the teacher leaks privileged information. On the other hand, we diagnose whether the
self-conditioned teacher approximates the optimal KL-regularized model, which is an assumption
behind prior self-distillation work (Shenfeld et al., 2026).
Remedies. Pan et al. (2026) and Yang et al. (2026) retain GRPO as a learning signal and use the
self-conditioned teacher only to modulate its per-token advantages. Because these methods do not
rely on the self-distillation loss alone, which is the setting we study, they fall outside the scope of
this work. Their reliance on GRPO is consistent with Harne et al. (2026), who question whether
the self-distillation objective on its own, without such auxiliary machinery, yields any performance
gains at all.

C QWEN3 THINKING MODES
Qwen3 models support a thinking and a non-thinking mode in the same model (Qwen Team, 2025).
For the flagship models (Qwen3-32B and Qwen3-235B-A22B), the two modes are merged during
post-training (Thinking Mode Fusion) by fine-tuning on a mixture of “thinking” and “non-thinking”
data. The smaller models (Qwen3 0.6B to 14B and Qwen3-30B-A3B) inherit both of these modes
through supervised fine-tuning and on-policy-distillation from the flagship models. Concretely, in
thinking mode, the model reasons inside a <think>. . . </think> block before responding. The
mode is toggled via the enable thinking
 argument of the chat template. When it is set to False,
an empty think block is prefilled in the assistant turn:

In non-thinking mode, the model is trained to respond directly after this empty thinking block.

E IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
All our experiments are run were run with 8xH100 nodes (with no multi-node parallelism), primar-
ily leveraging the vLLM inference engine (Kwon et al., 2023). We run our experiments on top of the
OPSD codebase written in TRL (von Werra et al., 2020) and the SDPO codebase which uses veRL
(Sheng et al., 2024). Our SDPO LCBv6 runs train for ∼40 hours, while the SDPO science and tool
use jobs took ∼3 hours. OPSD runs took up to ∼2 hours and GRPO runs on the OpenThoughts
dataset took up to ∼6 hours. OPSD thinking-on evaluations took up to ∼2.5 hours per dataset and
OPSD thinking-off evaluations took up to ∼1 hour per dataset. Our gradient similarity analyses took
up to ∼2 hours per training setting.
